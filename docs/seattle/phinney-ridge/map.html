        Seattle has two ways in. Which one fits depends on who's asking.

        If you have a disability, or directly care for someone who does

        Use SDOT's Curb Ramp Request form. This is the city's ADA program, and it builds ramps that people with disabilities ask for at places not already scheduled.

        • Each form can cover ramps at no more than two intersections. There's no limit on how many forms you send.
        • SDOT sends a first response within 15 days of verifying the request, and preliminary project scoping within 30 days.
        • Questions: DOT_ADAProgram@seattle.gov, (206) 386-1055, or toll-free (833) 414-5871.

        Open the SDOT Curb Ramp Request form

        From SDOT's Make an ADA Infrastructure Request page.

        Everyone else (strollers, carts, bikes, getting older)

        The ADA form is reserved for disability access, so send a general request to the city's Customer Service Bureau, which routes it to SDOT.

        • In the Find It, Fix It app, choose General Inquiry – Customer Service Bureau. There's no curb ramp category.
        • Or use the online request portal (same categories, no app needed).

        What to include

        Every corner on this map has a Copy request text button. It writes out the exact location, which crossing needs a ramp, and the evidence, plus a link back to that corner on this map. Paste it into the form, then attach a photo if you can.

        After you file

        Save your confirmation or tracking number and tell the group so we don't file duplicates and can follow up together. Please don't share your phone or email there.

        Ask together. Pick corners, and this page splits them into ready-to-file packets of two intersections each (SDOT's limit per form) and drafts a neighborhood letter.

        Or use Add to group request on any corner.

        More ways to push as a group

        • Split the packets. Neighbors with a disability (or caregivers) each file one packet on the ADA form. Everyone else sends the letter through the Customer Service Bureau.
        • Write your councilmember. Look up your district on the city's neighborhoods and council districts page, then send the letter below. One letter signed by many households carries more weight than many separate notes.
        • Speak at a public meeting. SDOT's Pedestrian Access Advisory Committee and the Seattle Pedestrian Advisory Board take public comment.
