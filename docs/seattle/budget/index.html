Where a yearly curb ramp budget should go first

The city can't build every missing ramp at once. Set how much it has each year, and this plan spends it on the worst areas first, finishing one area before moving to the next, so whole neighborhoods become walkable instead of a scattered few corners.

Paving a street and ramp projects should leave ramps behind; other street projects are the cheapest moment to add them, since the crew, traffic plan and design are already paid for. Ask each project to confirm.

        How the order is chosen

        • Worst first. Each square gets a score: how many of its intersections have a sidewalk but no curb ramp, with the extra weights you picked above. The highest score goes first.
        • Finish an area before starting the next. A trip is only as good as its worst crossing: three new ramps on a block with ten missing still leave a wheelchair user stuck. Building a whole area at once also means one crew, one traffic plan and one neighborhood notice instead of many. When a square's cost runs past the end of a year, it carries over and finishes first the next year.
        • The cost of an intersection is ramps per intersection times cost per ramp. The default for ramps per intersection is what SDOT has actually built: the average number of ramps it has recorded at intersections that have any. Four-way intersections usually need more, T-intersections fewer.
        • Scheduled work comes from dotMaps' paving and surface improvement layers, refreshed daily: projects that haven't ended and reach within 20 m of an intersection. Under federal guidance from 2013, repaving a street is an alteration that requires curb ramps at its crossings, so those intersections are left out of the budget by default. dotMaps only shows work about two years out; SDOT's longer paving plan isn't published as map data.
        • Neighborhood numbers (disability, age) come from the city's Community Reporting Areas, so every square in a neighborhood shares them.

        What this leaves out

        • It's not the city's whole ramp program. Under a 2017 settlement, Seattle builds or upgrades 1,250 ramps a year, most of them as part of paving and other projects, wherever those happen to be. This plan is for money the city can point at specific places.
        • Only ramps known to be missing. It counts the hotspot map's "no ramp" intersections. Where volunteers haven't surveyed, ramps are missing that this doesn't know about, and some corners on the list have been fixed since the photos were taken.
        • Not per corner. An intersection with one ramp counts as having ramps. The street pages go corner by corner.
        • No sidewalk, no ramp. Places without a sidewalk at all aren't counted; that's a bigger and more expensive problem.
        • It doesn't know about destinations. SDOT also weighs bus stops, schools, clinics and government buildings, and requests from people with disabilities. Use this to argue for where, then check the list against what people walk to.
