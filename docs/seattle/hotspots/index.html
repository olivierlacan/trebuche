Where Seattle's missing curb ramps cluster

Every intersection in the city, checked against SDOT's own records and volunteer street surveys. Missing ramps aren't spread evenly: they bunch up in a few neighborhoods. Find the one near you, and take it on with your neighbors.

    By neighborhood

    Neighborhoods are the city's Community Reporting Areas. "Surveyed" is the share of intersections Project Sidewalk volunteers have covered; where it's low, the count of missing ramps is likely too low. Select a column to sort.

    Organize your block

    The city does build curb ramps when people ask, and a group with specific, evidence-backed corners is hard to ignore.

    1. Find a few neighbors

      A building group chat, a Buy Nothing group, a PTA, a block party list, Nextdoor. Three to five people is plenty to start.

    2. Pick one street

      One stretch of 10 to 20 blocks is a manageable ask. Choose the hotspot street people actually walk: to a school, a park, a bus stop or a grocery store.

    3. Get a street page

      Use "Organize this one" on a hotspot to ask for a Trebuche page: every corner checked one by one, a map, and copy-ready request text. Dayton Ave N shows what you get.

    4. File, together

      Neighbors with a disability (or their caregivers) file SDOT's ADA form, two intersections each. Everyone else signs one letter to SDOT and your council district's member. How to ask →

    How this is counted, and what it misses

    • No ramp means SDOT's curb ramp inventory has no built ramp at the intersection, and either SDOT recorded "no ramp" or Project Sidewalk volunteers labeled a missing ramp there and labeled no ramps.
    • Only intersections with a sidewalk are counted (from SDOT's sidewalk inventory). Much of north Seattle and parts of the south end have no sidewalks at all; that's a bigger problem than ramps, and it doesn't show here.
    • Unknown means no record either way: SDOT has nothing, and volunteers haven't labeled it. Low survey coverage, as in parts of Southeast and West Seattle, makes an area look better than it is.
    • Imagery ages. Project Sidewalk's photos are mostly from 2018 to 2022, so some of these corners have been fixed since. Street pages check every corner against OpenStreetMap too, and flag where sources disagree.
    • Counted per intersection, not per corner. A partly ramped intersection counts as having ramps.
