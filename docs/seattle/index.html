How to ask Seattle for a curb ramp

Seattle has two channels, and which one fits depends on who's asking. Checked against seattle.gov on November 1, 2026.

If you have a disability, or directly care for someone who does

Use SDOT's Curb Ramp Request form (the city's ADA program). Each form covers ramps at no more than two intersections, and there's no limit on how many you send. SDOT responds within 15 days of verifying a request, with preliminary scoping within 30. Questions: DOT_ADAProgram@seattle.gov, (206) 386-1055, or (833) 414-5871.

Curb Ramp Request form →

Everyone else

The ADA program is limited to disability access, so other requests (strollers, carts, getting older) go to the Customer Service Bureau, which routes them to SDOT. In the Find It, Fix It app choose General Inquiry; there's no curb ramp category.

Online request portal →

As a group

Neighbors who qualify file the ADA form in two-intersection packets; others send one signed letter to SDOT and your councilmember. Public comment is also open at SDOT's Pedestrian Access Advisory Committee and the Seattle Pedestrian Advisory Board.

What a standard Seattle curb ramp looks like

Seattle builds curb ramps to the City's Standard Plans for Municipal Construction (2026 edition, Seattle Public Utilities and SDOT). Knowing the standard helps you ask for something specific and check what gets built. Links open the exact page of the plans PDF (about 30 MB).

Plan 422a: perpendicular ramp

The usual corner ramp, and what most Dayton Ave N ramps are recorded as ("Type 422A"). A ramp at least 4 ft wide and no steeper than 8.3%, a landing at least 4 ft deep at the top, and sloped wings (10% max) on each side. Where the ramp crosses a planting strip, side curbs replace the wings and the ramp is at least 5 ft wide.

Plan 422a →

Plan 422k: general rules

Two ramps at each corner, lined up with the crossing. A yellow detectable warning panel of raised domes, at least 2 ft deep, across the full ramp at the curb. A clear 4 ft by 4 ft area at the bottom. Poles and hydrants at least 1 ft from the ramp; no utility covers in the ramp or landing.

Plan 422k →

Plans 420 and 712: sidewalk and crosswalk

Sidewalks at least 6 ft wide around corners (420). Marked crosswalks in Seattle's "ladder style": pairs of white bars parallel to the curb, with the ramp's clear area inside the crosswalk lines (712).

Plan 712 →

The other ramp types are parallel ramps (422b, 422c), directional ramps (422d, 422e), a shared diagonal ramp (422f, only when nothing else fits), blended transitions (422g) and cut-throughs at traffic islands (422h). SDOT's engineers pick the type for each corner; requests don't need to.

What Seattle's data shows, and what it misses

Trebuche uses three public sources for Seattle. Each is useful; none is right everywhere.

SDOT curb ramp inventory

Every ramp the city has recorded, with install dates, condition and planned work orders. Where a ramp was never built, there's usually no record at all, so a missing ramp looks the same as an unsurveyed corner.

Inventory data →

Project Sidewalk Seattle

University of Washington volunteers labeling street-level imagery, including "no curb ramp". It catches gaps the city never logged, but much of its imagery is from 2018 to 2022.

Label map →

OpenStreetMap

Community map data, largely from the University of Washington's OpenSidewalks mapping, recording whether each crossing's curb is raised or lowered. That's what tells us which crossing at a corner lacks a ramp.

OpenStreetMap →

Things we found checking Seattle's maps

  • Placeholder records drawn as built ramps. At Dayton Ave N & N 79th St, the inventory holds eight records with no type and zero width, tied to a 2022 project marked "on hold". SDOT's public curb ramp maps include them in layers named "Built Curb Ramps" and "No Data for Compliance", although no ramps had been built. By November 2026 the ramps were built at all four corners, as a neighbor found in person, but the records still show the project on hold.
  • Stalled work is hidden from the planned-ramps map. SDOT's Planned Curb Ramp app only shows work marked "planned" or "planned reconstruction". The on-hold project has a blank status, so it doesn't appear.
  • Old imagery misses new ramps. Project Sidewalk scores Dayton & N 63rd St as having no ramps, from 2022 imagery; SDOT built them in August 2026.
